\title{How much water does a data center cooling tower really use?\\
A multi-method, physics-based evaporation and blowdown framework for EnergyPlus}

\author{Liang Zhang}
\address{Department of Civil and Architectural Engineering and Mechanics, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, USA \tbd{confirm affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Water use is becoming a siting and permitting constraint for data centers, yet building energy simulation
estimates cooling tower water with simplified relations: a fixed loss per kelvin of range, or Merkel's
assumption that exit air is saturated, combined with a fixed number of cycles of concentration.
We present an open framework, implemented as an EnergyPlus Python plugin, that evaluates eight
evaporation methods---from bounding and empirical relations to the effectiveness model of Braun et al.\ and the full
Poppe heat and mass transfer model with supersaturated air---at the same simulated operating point every
timestep, together with a blowdown model driven by makeup-water chemistry (constituent limits, Langelier
saturation index, silica solubility). The Poppe implementation is verified against an independent
boundary-value solution of the conservation equations to $<10^{-4}$\,\%.
For a fixed counterflow tower at full load, empirical methods deviate from Poppe by $-$39 to +117\,\% across
ambient conditions, and saturated-exit methods, including the EnergyPlus default, by $-$18 to +11\,\%:
they overpredict in hot--dry air and underpredict in cold--humid air, where Poppe predicts fogging exit air.
Applied to a 1\,MW water-cooled data center in 14 climates (ASHRAE zones 0A--6A), the EnergyPlus default
underestimates annual evaporation by 3.7--9.3\,\%, most in cold climates; the EnergyPlus loss-factor method,
used as documented, overestimates it by 4--64\,\%. Evaporation per unit heat rejected ranges from 1.05 to
1.65\,L/kWh$_{th}$ across climates, and makeup-water chemistry changes total makeup by $-$13 to +22\,\%, as much as
the choice of evaporation physics. The framework also models hybrid adiabatic dry coolers by imposing pad-cooled air on EnergyPlus fluid coolers
through EMS actuators. With wetting above 28\,\textdegree C, the hybrid plant keeps its chillers within their rated
range at every site and uses 14--100\,\% less water than the evaporative tower, but always-wet operation in the
hottest, driest climate uses more water than the tower; water spent per kWh of electricity saved is 100--240\,L/kWh.
We also document five defects in EnergyPlus heat-rejection models, one of which inflates loss-factor evaporation
100-fold and another fluid-cooler air flow about 1000-fold.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================
Data centers convert almost all of their electricity into heat, and most large facilities reject it through
evaporative cooling towers. Their direct water use is therefore a function of heat load, climate, makeup-water
quality and plant control \citep{mytton2021data,shehabi2016united}. As new campuses concentrate in water-stressed
regions, operators, utilities and permitting agencies increasingly need credible hourly and annual water estimates at
the design stage, and metrics such as water usage effectiveness (WUE) \citep{greengrid2011wue} are reported publicly.

Building energy simulation is the natural tool for such estimates, because it already resolves the hourly heat
rejected by the plant. EnergyPlus, the most widely used open engine, computes tower evaporation either with a constant
loss factor or by assuming saturated exit air (Merkel's assumption), and blowdown with a fixed concentration ratio
\citep{doe2022engref}. These simplifications are well known in the cooling tower literature: Merkel's method
neglects the Lewis factor, the water lost by evaporation and the real exit-air state \citep{merkel1925verdunstungskuhlung,kloppers2005critical}.
\citet{kloppers2005critical,kloppers2005cooling} showed that Merkel and e-NTU methods predict heat rejection
close to the Poppe method \citep{poppe1991berechnung} but differ from it in predicted water evaporation, with the difference
depending on ambient conditions \tbd{check exact claim and magnitude in K\&K 2005 before submission}. These findings have not been carried into building energy simulation, where water estimates feed design
decisions, nor have they been combined with water chemistry, which sets blowdown---often a quarter or more of makeup.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An open, multi-method framework that evaluates eight evaporation models on the same EnergyPlus operating point
every timestep, so differences isolate evaporation physics (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item A blowdown model driven by makeup-water chemistry, including constituent limits, the Langelier saturation index
and temperature-dependent silica solubility (Section~\ref{sec:chem}).
\item Verification of every model, including an independent boundary-value solution of the Poppe equations
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}), and a climate-general map of where each simplification fails
(Section~\ref{sec:map}).
\item A multi-climate case study of a representative water-cooled data center and an analysis of
water-accounting defects in EnergyPlus heat-rejection models (Sections~\ref{sec:case}--\ref{sec:results}).
\item A hybrid adiabatic dry cooler model coupled to EnergyPlus, and the water--energy trade-off of its
wet/dry control across climates (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Accounting mode: one operating point, many evaporation models}
At the end of each system timestep, the plugin reads, for every tower, the water inlet and outlet temperatures,
water mass flow, heat rejected, air-flow ratio, operating cells and fan part-load ratio, plus the outdoor dry-bulb,
wet-bulb, humidity ratio and pressure. The air mass flow is reconstructed from the design air flow and the fan regime
(Section~\ref{sec:airflow}). Every evaporation method is then evaluated at this operating point. EnergyPlus' thermal
solution is not changed. A rating mode, available in the standalone library, instead predicts the cold-water
temperature from a fill Merkel number for each method; it is used for the climate map and for future validation
against manufacturer data.

Table~\ref{tab:methods} lists the eight methods; full equations are given in the supplementary technical reference
(docs/METHODS.md).

\paragraph{Poppe model} With water temperature as the independent variable, the humidity ratio, air enthalpy and
Merkel number obey \citep{kloppers2005critical}
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\omega}{dT_w} = \frac{c_{pw}\,(\dot m_w/\dot m_a)(\omega_{sw}-\omega)}{D},\quad
\frac{di_{ma}}{dT_w} = \frac{\dot m_w c_{pw}}{\dot m_a}\left[1+\frac{c_{pw}T_w(\omega_{sw}-\omega)}{D}\right],\quad
\frac{dMe_P}{dT_w} = \frac{c_{pw}}{D},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
D = (i_{masw}-i_{ma}) + (Le_f-1)\left[i_{masw}-i_{ma}-(\omega_{sw}-\omega)i_v\right] - (\omega_{sw}-\omega)c_{pw}T_w,
\end{equation}
with a corresponding form for supersaturated air, and the Lewis factor of \citet{bosnjakovic1965technical}. Because both water
temperatures are known in accounting mode, the equations are integrated from the bottom to the top of the fill (RK4,
ten steps) and the Merkel number is an output; no fill correlation is required. The unknown exit humidity enters the
local water flow and is found by fixed-point iteration.

\paragraph{Braun e-NTU model} The air-side effectiveness of the operating point gives the number of transfer units in
closed form, and the exit humidity follows the effective-saturation relation of \citet{braun1989effectiveness}. This
model is thermodynamically consistent with the EnergyPlus \texttt{CoolingTower:VariableSpeed:Merkel} object.

\subsection{Air flow reconstruction}\label{sec:airflow}
Physical evaporation models need the actual air mass flow. For \texttt{CoolingTower:VariableSpeed:Merkel} it is
the reported fan-speed ratio times the design air flow and the fraction of cells operating; in free convection, where
EnergyPlus reports zero air flow while rejecting heat, the sized free-convection air flow is used. For the regression-based
\texttt{CoolingTower:VariableSpeed} models, EnergyPlus reports the free-convection \emph{capacity} fraction as an
air-flow ratio when the fan is off or cycling; the plugin replaces it with the time average of the minimum-speed and
free-convection air flows weighted by the fan part-load ratio. Because the Merkel model ties air flow to a $UA$
relation, it is used in the case study.

\paragraph{Operating points beyond the thermodynamic limit}
EnergyPlus' Merkel tower linearises the saturation enthalpy (Braun's $c_s$) and selects the lowest fan speed that meets
the condenser setpoint. With an oversized tower at part load this places the exit air within a fraction of a percent of
the saturated enthalpy at the hot-water temperature. Poppe, which accounts for $Le_f<1$ and the enthalpy of the evaporated
water, can then find no positive driving force near the top of the fill. In that case the plugin re-solves the point with
the smallest air-flow increase (from 1\,\% to 50\,\%) that makes it feasible and records the factor. Near the pinch,
evaporation is insensitive to air flow: for a representative point, +2\,\% air changes Poppe evaporation by $<0.1$\,\% and
+20\,\% by 1.4\,\%.

\subsection{Blowdown from makeup-water chemistry}\label{sec:chem}
Blowdown follows from the dissolved-solids balance, $B = E/(N-1) - D$, where $N$ is the cycles of concentration.
Instead of a fixed $N$, the plugin sets $N$ to the most restrictive of: constituent limits (silica, calcium hardness,
chloride, TDS), product limits, a calcium carbonate limit $LSI\le LSI_{\max}$ evaluated at the hot-water temperature
\citep{langelier1936analytical}, for which $N_{LSI} = 10^{(LSI_{\max}-pH+pH_s(1))/1.9}$ in closed form, and amorphous-silica
solubility at the cold-water temperature \citep{fournier1977chemical}. Three modes are available: fixed (EnergyPlus),
constituent (constant), and dynamic (re-evaluated every timestep).

\section{Where do simplified methods fail? A climate-general map}\label{sec:map}
%======================================================================
A counterflow tower was sized with the Poppe model for a \SI{3.9}{K} approach and \SI{5.6}{K} range at
\SI{35}{\celsius}/\SI{25.6}{\celsius} design conditions and $L/G=1.2$ ($Me_P = 1.27$). With fill, $L/G$ and range fixed,
the cold-water temperature was solved with Poppe over a grid of dry-bulb ($-5$ to \SI{46}{\celsius}) and relative humidity
(5--100\,\%), and all methods were evaluated at each point (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}).

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_method_map_physical}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig_method_map_empirical}
\caption{Evaporation of each method relative to Poppe for a fixed tower at full load. Top: physics-based methods
($\pm$20\,\% scale). Bottom: empirical methods ($\pm$120\,\% scale). Lines: outdoor wet-bulb.}\label{fig:map}
\end{figure}

Three patterns emerge. (i) Saturated-exit methods, including the EnergyPlus default, overpredict by up to
11\,\% in hot--dry air, where real exit air is unsaturated, and underpredict by up to 18\,\% in cold--humid
air, where Poppe predicts supersaturated exit air carrying liquid mist. (ii) The Braun e-NTU model underpredicts
everywhere by 3--18\,\%. (iii) The Lewis factor alone changes evaporation by only $-$4.2 to +1.8\,\%. Empirical methods
are unusable outside the conditions they implicitly assume: the EnergyPlus loss factor of 0.2\,\%/K overpredicts by up to
117\,\% in cold weather, and the latent-only bound underpredicts by up to 32\,\% in hot--dry weather, where
sensible heat flows from the air into the water.

\section{Case study: a water-cooled data center}\label{sec:case}
%======================================================================
The host model (Table~\ref{tab:dcmodel}) is a single-storey facility with two 500\,m$^2$ data halls and
1.0\,MW of air-cooled IT equipment (\texttt{ElectricEquipment:ITE:AirCooled}) operating at 70--90\,\% CPU
utilisation (mean IT power 861\,kW, 7,540\,MWh/yr). Each hall is served by a recirculating CRAH-type air
handler with a chilled-water coil and variable-speed fan, supplying 20\,\textdegree C air (ASHRAE class A1).
Three water-cooled variable-speed centrifugal chillers (Carrier 19XR curve set from the EnergyPlus dataset), each
sized at 50\,\% of the design load (N+1), supply 10\,\textdegree C chilled water. Heat is rejected by a three-cell
\texttt{CoolingTower:VariableSpeed:Merkel} sized for a 3.9\,K approach and 5.6\,K range at the site's 0.4\,\%
evaporation design wet-bulb; condenser water follows outdoor wet-bulb plus 3.9\,K between 18 and 29.4\,\textdegree C.
For every site, design days, site data, tower design air state and condenser design temperature are replaced with that
site's ASHRAE values, so the plant is sized for its climate. Weather is TMYx 2011--2025 for 14 sites spanning ASHRAE
climate zones 0A to 6A. Drift is 0.001\,\% of circulating flow. Annual PUE is 1.24--1.28 across sites, with zero unmet
zone hours. Parameter sources and assumptions are listed in the supplementary model documentation.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Evaporation by method and climate}
Table~\ref{tab:sites} and Fig.~\ref{fig:dcdev} summarise the annual results. The EnergyPlus SaturatedExit replica
reproduces EnergyPlus' own evaporation within 0.2\,\% at every site. Against Poppe, the EnergyPlus default
underestimates annual evaporation at all 14 sites, by 3.7\,\% (Phoenix) to 9.3\,\% (Montreal). The other
saturated-exit and e-NTU formulations behave similarly ($-$2.2 to $-$7.3\,\%), and the Lewis factor alone accounts for
0.1--2.1\,\%. The deviation is largest in cold climates and smallest in hot--dry ones; at the timestep level it
reaches $-$29\,\% at outdoor temperatures below $-$20\,\textdegree C (Fig.~\ref{fig:dcts}), where the Poppe exit air is
supersaturated. Exit air carrying more than 0.1\,g/kg of liquid occurs in 400\,h (Mumbai) to 7,200\,h (Dublin) per year.

Unlike the full-load map of Section~\ref{sec:map}, saturated-exit methods do not overpredict in hot--dry climates here.
The data-center tower is oversized (N+1) and runs at part load near its thermodynamic limit, so its exit air is close to
saturation at the hot-water temperature even in dry weather. Whether a simplified method over- or underpredicts thus
depends on how close the tower operates to the pinch, not only on climate.

Empirical methods do not follow the climate signal at all (Fig.~\ref{fig:dcmonthly}). The loss factor of 0.2\,\%/K, as
documented, is nearly constant through the year and overestimates annual evaporation by 4\,\% (Riyadh) to 64\,\%
(Dublin); the rule of thumb ranges from $-$20 to +26\,\% and the latent-only estimate from $-$11 to +40\,\%. They are
closest to Poppe in hot--dry climates, where sensible heat flows from the air into the water.

\subsection{Blowdown, makeup and WUE}
Evaporation per unit heat rejected ranges from 1.05\,L/kWh$_{th}$ (Dublin) to 1.65\,L/kWh$_{th}$ (Riyadh). With
blowdown, WUE ranges from 1.66 to 2.63\,L/kWh (Fig.~\ref{fig:wue}). Replacing EnergyPlus' fixed four cycles by
constituent-limited cycles and Poppe evaporation raises WUE by up to 6\,\%. Across the three illustrative makeup sources
and the constituent and dynamic (LSI and silica solubility) chemistry modes, makeup at a given site varies from $-$13\,\%
to +22\,\% around that estimate. Makeup-water quality is therefore as important as evaporation physics for annual
water use, and needs measured utility data in any site assessment.

\subsection{Water-accounting defects in EnergyPlus}
Analysis of EnergyPlus v22.2 (unchanged on the development branch in October 2026) identified five defects, documented
with reproductions in the supplementary material: (1) towers that are not running still book evaporation, drift and
blowdown computed from a stale air-flow ratio (8.65\,m$^3$/yr in a stock example with zero heat rejected);
(2) the Merkel tower books no evaporation in free convection; (3) regression-based variable-speed towers use a capacity
fraction as an air-flow ratio in the fan-off regime; and (4) the \texttt{LossFactor} input, documented in percent per
kelvin, is applied as a fraction, so the documented default evaporates 100 times the intended amount (20\,\%/K;
up to 24\,\% of circulating flow in a stock example); and (5) fluid-cooler air flow is autosized as nominal capacity in
watts divided by the design temperature difference times 4, read as m$^3$/s, about 1000 times a physical value
(14,061\,m$^3$/s for a 58.6\,kW stock example). Defects (1)--(3) did not occur in the data-center case study,
whose tower never idles or enters free convection; they matter for multi-tower plants and lightly loaded towers.

\section{Hybrid adiabatic dry coolers}\label{sec:hybrid}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Model}
Hybrid (adiabatic) dry coolers are the main water-saving alternative to towers in new data centers. EnergyPlus has
dry fluid coolers but no water-side hybrid cooler, so the plugin builds one: a bank of \texttt{FluidCooler:TwoSpeed}
objects shares one outdoor-air node, and in wet mode the plugin overrides that node's dry-bulb with the pad-leaving
temperature, keeping the wet-bulb, through EMS actuators at the start of each timestep. EnergyPlus then simulates the
coils with pre-cooled air, and a condenser setpoint manager that follows the node lowers the condenser water
temperature, so the chillers see the benefit. Pad cooling follows adiabatic saturation,
\begin{equation}
T_{pre} = T_{db} - \varepsilon\,(T_{db}-T_{wb}),\qquad i(T_{db},\omega_i) + (\omega_{pre}-\omega_i)c_{pw}T_{wb} = i(T_{pre},\omega_{pre}),
\end{equation}
with pad effectiveness $\varepsilon=0.85$ (assumption, typical of rigid media). Evaporation is
$E=\sum_k f_k\dot m_{a,k}(\omega_{pre}-\omega_i)$ over the fan states of the timestep, recovered exactly from the
coolers' reported fan power, and bleed follows the chemistry model of Section~\ref{sec:chem}. Unlike the tower
analysis, this plugin changes the EnergyPlus solution.

\subsection{Case study and control strategies}
The data-center model of Section~\ref{sec:case} was given a hybrid heat-rejection plant: three 50\,\% fluid coolers
(N+1) sized for wet operation at the 0.4\,\% dry-bulb design condition (5.6\,K approach and range, 8\,K design air
temperature rise, hard-sized air flows), a condenser setpoint of pad-leaving air plus 5.6\,K (23--40\,\textdegree C), and
three 50\,\% inlet-vane centrifugal chillers (Trane CVHE curve set, rated COP 5.38). No chiller curve set in the
EnergyPlus dataset is valid above 41.1\,\textdegree C leaving condenser water; hours beyond that limit, where EnergyPlus
clamps the curves and underestimates chiller energy, are reported for every case. Seven control strategies were run
at all 14 sites on identical hardware: dry only, always wet (above 5\,\textdegree C), and wet when the outdoor
dry-bulb exceeds $T_{on}=$ 16, 20, 24, 28 or 32\,\textdegree C (2\,K hysteresis). Because the tower and hybrid plants use
different chiller curve sets, energy is compared only among hybrid strategies, and the tower enters as a water
reference.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} and Figs.~\ref{fig:hybtrade}--\ref{fig:hybwue} summarise the results. Three findings stand out.

\emph{Wet operation is primarily a capacity measure.} Dry-only, the chillers run beyond their rated condenser range
for up to 3,285\,h/yr (Riyadh), 2,800\,h (Phoenix) and 1,170--1,880\,h in Dallas, Houston, Mumbai and Singapore. Wetting above
28\,\textdegree C reduces this to at most 5\,h/yr at every site.

\emph{Saving electricity with pad water is expensive.} Relative to dry operation, wetting above 28\,\textdegree C saves 0--1.7\,\%
of facility electricity at a marginal cost of 100--122\,L of water per kWh saved, and always-wet operation 107--241\,L/kWh.
These figures overstate the cost somewhat, because the dry baseline's chiller energy is underestimated where curves clamp.

\emph{Control decides whether a hybrid saves water.} With wetting above 28\,\textdegree C, site water is 14\,\% (Riyadh),
31\,\% (Phoenix) and 41\,\% (Las Vegas) lower than the evaporative tower, 60--85\,\% lower in hot--humid climates, and
93--100\,\% lower in cool climates. Always-wet operation in Riyadh uses 9\,\% \emph{more} water than the tower, because the
pads cool all the air the dry coil moves, which is more air than a wet tower needs for the same heat.

\section{Discussion}
%======================================================================
\paragraph{Which method to use} For annual totals in building simulation, saturated-exit and e-NTU methods are within
about 10\,\% of Poppe, biased low in cold climates and at part load near the pinch. Loss-factor and rule-of-thumb
estimates should not be used for siting or permitting: their error changes sign with climate and exceeds 60\,\% in
cool, humid climates. Where hourly water matters (peak demand on a utility, drought curtailment, plume abatement),
Poppe is preferable because its error relative to saturated exit is concentrated in cold, humid hours.

\paragraph{Limitations} (i) Accounting mode: the thermal solution and air flow are EnergyPlus', so any bias in its tower
model propagates; near the pinch the plugin adjusts air flow by up to 3\,\% on average to keep the Poppe solution
physical. (ii) Water chemistry inputs are illustrative placeholders; treatment limits and the empirical LSI form need
site data and vendor limits. (iii) No measured validation yet: manufacturer performance tables and metered makeup and
blowdown are needed. (iv) One plant configuration was studied, without water-side economizer, and with drift from a
single eliminator assumption.

\paragraph{Hybrid coolers} The hybrid results argue for controls that wet only to keep chillers within their rated
condenser range, not to save electricity: below the capacity limit, each kWh saved costs on the order of 100\,L of water.
The hybrid analysis assumes a constant pad effectiveness, no pad pressure drop, perfect knowledge of the current
dry-bulb, placeholder water chemistry, and uses a chiller curve set whose rated range ends at 41.1\,\textdegree C; a
high-lift chiller characterisation and manufacturer pad data are needed before the energy side is used quantitatively.

\paragraph{Outlook} The verified Poppe and e-NTU rating functions allow a full physics-based tower as an EnergyPlus
user-defined plant component; demand-based wet/dry control (wetting only when the condenser setpoint cannot be held)
and spray and wetted-coil hybrids are natural extensions of the hybrid model.

\section{Conclusions}
%======================================================================
\begin{enumerate}
\item A multi-method EnergyPlus plugin evaluates eight evaporation models on the same operating point every timestep,
with chemistry-driven blowdown; the Poppe implementation agrees with an independent boundary-value solution to
$<10^{-4}$\,\%.
\item For a full-load tower, saturated-exit methods deviate from Poppe by $-$18 to +11\,\% depending on ambient
conditions; empirical methods by $-$39 to +117\,\%.
\item In a 1\,MW data center across 14 climates, the EnergyPlus default underestimates annual evaporation by
3.7--9.3\,\%, most in cold climates; the documented loss-factor method overestimates it by 4--64\,\%.
\item Makeup-water chemistry changes annual makeup by $-$13 to +22\,\%, comparable to the choice of evaporation physics.
\item A hybrid adiabatic dry cooler with wetting above 28\,\textdegree C keeps chillers within their rated range at all
sites and uses 14--100\,\% less water than the tower; always-wet operation can use more water than a tower, and saving
electricity with pad water costs about 100--240\,L/kWh.
\item Five defects were identified in EnergyPlus heat-rejection models; one inflates loss-factor evaporation
100-fold and another autosizes fluid-cooler air flow about 1000-fold.
\end{enumerate}
